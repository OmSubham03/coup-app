Choose your mystery

  1. Open Play 20 Questions now, choose the question budget, and create a room.
  2. Share the five-character room code with friends. Use display names, then have the host start with at least two players.
  3. The host is the first word-giver and selects a category and secret word. Guessers see the category, not the answer.

The word-giver is another player, not an AI. The host may enable public-room discovery. A saved session in the same tab takes priority over a new game link.

Work together inside the budget

Guessers take turns asking a question. The giver replies with yes, no, or a short clarification, and the next guesser takes over. Read earlier answers so the group does not spend another turn confirming the same clue.

How the counter works here

In this implementation, the displayed question counter advances after a full rotation of guessers, not after each individual player's question. An incorrect guess during the asking phase also advances the counter once. With just one guesser, each answered question completes a rotation.

Any non-giver can make a guess during the asking phase; it does not have to be their question turn. You must wait while the giver is answering a question or judging another guess.

Verdicts and the final round

The giver marks a guess correct or wrong. The game does not require automatic exact-text matching, so the giver can recognize a reasonable spelling or equivalent answer. A correct verdict ends the round with a guessers' win and reveals the answer.

Once the budget is exhausted, each guesser gets one final guess in turn order. The giver still verifies every attempt. If all final guesses fail, the giver wins. Starting the next round rotates the giver role to the next player.

Make the clues do the work

The category is Animal and the secret is elephant. One guesser asks whether it is a mammal, another whether it usually lives on land, and a third whether it is larger than a person. The giver answers yes to all three. That complete rotation advances the counter once.

A player then guesses giraffe. The giver marks it wrong, consuming another counter step. A later question about a trunk points to elephant; the giver can accept a clear equivalent answer instead of rejecting it for a small spelling mistake.

Reduce the possibilities before naming one

  • Start broad: living or non-living, real or fictional, indoors or outdoors.
  • Ask one clear question at a time. A combined question can make a yes/no answer ambiguous.
  • Use the shared history to avoid contradictions in your theory.
  • As giver, choose a recognizable answer and apply the same standard to every player's guesses.
