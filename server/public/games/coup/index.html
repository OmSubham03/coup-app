Get your table together

  1. Choose Play Coup now. On the setup screen, create a Standard room with Ambassador or an Inquisitor room.
  2. Choose a display name and share the five-character room code with your friends. They select Coup, enter the code, and choose the matching variant.
  3. The host starts when at least two players have joined. Rooms are private by default; the host can enable Public room to appear in available rooms.

No download, payment, or Google sign-in is required. A saved game in the same browser tab takes priority over a new game link, so following this guide does not silently replace an existing session.

The rules used by TimeCrusher

Every player starts with two coins and two hidden influence cards, including in a two-player game. The starting player is selected randomly. Losing influence reveals one of your cards; losing both eliminates you. The last player with hidden influence wins.

Actions that do not require a role

  • Income: take one coin. It cannot be blocked or challenged.
  • Foreign Aid: take two coins, unless an opponent blocks by claiming Duke.
  • Coup: pay seven coins and choose an opponent to lose one influence. It cannot be blocked or challenged. At ten or more coins, you must Coup instead of taking another action.

Bluffing, challenges, and blocks

You may claim a role you do not hold. Other living players can challenge a role claim, and blocks can be challenged too. If you cannot show a hidden card of the claimed role, you lose influence. If you can prove the role, the challenger loses influence, and your proven card is shuffled back into the deck and replaced.

An influence loss does not let you invent a new card: choose from the cards you still hold. Read the current action and response prompts before passing, blocking, or challenging; an assassination challenge can be much riskier than letting a simple Tax resolve.

The Inquisitor variant

Inquisitor replaces Ambassador. Inquire draws one card and returns one while preserving your influence count. Interrogate targets another player: you see a selected hidden card and can force it to be replaced from the deck. Inquisitor, rather than Ambassador, can block stealing. The other roles and basic coin actions remain the same.

A challenge changes more than the coin count

Mira has two coins and claims Duke to take Tax. Dev challenges. Mira really has a hidden Duke, so she proves it. Dev chooses an influence to lose; Mira returns the proven Duke to the deck and receives a replacement.

After the remaining response resolves, a successful Tax brings Mira to five coins. Dev has learned that the original claim was true, not that Mira still has a Duke. Her replacement could be a different role. On a later turn, two more coins would put a seven-coin Coup within reach.

Give your bluffs a reason to be believed

  • Watch coins first. Seven coins creates a guaranteed threat; ten forces its use. A quiet player with a large stack can be more dangerous than a loud claimant.
  • Challenge with a purpose. Risking your last influence over one extra coin is very different from stopping an action that could eliminate you.
  • Remember exchanges. A successful proof or exchange changes hidden information. Do not treat an earlier reveal as permanent knowledge.
  • Choose the loss deliberately. Keeping a useful blocker may buy more time than keeping an aggressive role when an opponent is already targeting you.
